APRS Coverage, Tracking & Telemetry

What the APRS packet logs tell us about radio coverage, where the rovers went, and how the new Voyager trackers behaved — the 2026 Prouty Ultimate (Fri) and Bike Event (Sat).

Source: aprsis capture stream, 35,134 packets · window 2026-07-10 00:00 – 2026-07-11 23:59 UTC · analysis Nov 2026

Every APRS packet heard during the event was captured to a local database as it arrived. After the event we replayed that capture to answer three questions: where did our digipeater/igate network actually have coverage, where did the rovers go, and how did the trackers themselves perform — including the new Microsat Voyager units that report their own GPS state. This page summarizes the findings; the maps and raw tables are linked as downloads, and the analysis code is in a repository (see Tooling).

One capture caveat up front

The capture pipeline itself went silent once, for about 11 minutes on Saturday (15:03–15:14 UTC). Any apparent "silence" from a tracker in that window is ours, not theirs. Everything below accounts for it.

1.Coverage & gaps

Coverage here means RF reception by our network, mapped from the path of every packet: a station is credited with a packet when it digipeated it (CALL*) or gated it to the internet directly off-air (qAR/qAO). We plot the transmitting rover's position for each such packet, so each receiver's dot cloud is the real-world footprint of what it could hear. Holes in a cloud are that station's coverage gaps.

Each of the seven fixed stations gets its own color, used for both its reception dots and a ring marking its site. Traffic the stations relayed from each other (infrastructure beacons) is separated into its own layer so it doesn't masquerade as field coverage, and igates are credited only for packets they actually heard on RF, not ones they received over the internet from another igate.

Reading the footprints: solid dots are packets a station heard first-hand (the rover itself); faded dots are packets it only got after another digi had already repeated them. A station whose cloud is mostly faded was leaning on the network to reach the route rather than hearing it directly — useful for spotting where a single site is carrying more than its share.

Dropouts are mostly coverage, not tracker faults

Every rover shows many multi-minute stretches where we heard nothing from it (15–25 each, some 30–60 min). Because the pattern appears on all trackers at once, it reflects terrain and capture coverage along the remote northern route far more than simultaneous tracker failure. Distinguishing a true tracker outage from a dead spot requires cross-referencing the coverage map above — a dropout where no station heard anyone is a coverage hole, not a tracker fault. (This cross-tag is a planned refinement.)

2.Where the rovers went

Each rover's track is rebuilt from its own position beacons, exported as a KMZ comparable to an aprs.fi export but without the download quota — and with a dot at every received packet, so the spacing of dots along a road shows where coverage was dense (dots close together) versus sparse (long gaps between dots on a road the rover clearly drove).

3.New tracker functionality — GPS lock reporting

This year's rovers carried Microsat APRS Voyager units, which do something the old trackers did not: they announce their own GPS state on the air, sending a status of "GPS Lock Lost" when they lose the satellites and "GPS Locked" when they reacquire. That turns a guessing game into a measurement — we can say exactly when a rover went dark, roughly where, and for how long, by pairing the two statuses.

This is a genuinely useful new signal. Previously a frozen position could mean a parked vehicle or a dead GPS and we couldn't tell; now the tracker tells us. Across the event the fleet reported 27 GPS-loss episodes, nearly all brief:

RoverGPS lossesLongestPattern
W1CMS-89~2 minScattered all day; many very short (12–48 s)

Two readings stand out. W1CMS-11's losses are the operationally interesting ones: four of them ran 8 to 16 minutes and all clustered in the higher-elevation northern section of the route in the afternoon — a clear terrain-shadowing signature, the kind of place where a rover would appear stuck on the map while actually still moving. W1CMS-8 lost lock most often (nine times) but almost always recovered within seconds, which reads more like an install or antenna issue than terrain (see the telemetry note below). The short, same-spot bursts from W1CMS-7 and W1CMS-9 are consistent with a specific shadowed location rather than a tracker problem.

Why this matters for next year

Because the Voyagers self-report, a frozen position without an accompanying "GPS Lock Lost" now points somewhere other than the sky — a wedged tracker, a timing issue, or a stale buffer — and is worth investigating separately. The GPS-loss map (below) shows exactly where on the course these dropouts happen, which is the input for deciding where coverage or a fill-in digi would help most.

4.Tracker health & telemetry

The Voyagers also beacon analog telemetry (T# packets). Each unit transmits its own calibration, so the raw 0–255 counts decode to real units: channel 1 is input voltage at 0.075 V/count, channel 2 is temperature (0.5·raw − 64 °C). Battery voltage is the useful one — it tells us whether a rover's electrical system held up.

The fleet was healthy on power — everything sat in the normal 12–15 V band for a vehicle system (alternator charging up near 14.5–15, engine-off resting ~12.5), and nobody came close to a low-battery condition. The brief ~12.15–12.2 V dips on three units are ordinary engine-off rest stops, not sags.

W1CMS-8 is the one to look at

It is the only unit trending down (last 12.82 V) with no strong alternator-charging peak, and it had the most GPS losses (nine) and a frozen episode. Low or unstable supply voltage can itself make a tracker's GPS and RF flaky, so the charging/wiring on that install is worth an eyeball before next year — the voltage trend and the GPS behavior point at the same suspect.

5.Tooling

All of the analysis is reproducible from the capture database with a small set of standalone Python tools, kept in a git repository (prouty-aprs-tools). They fall into the same two buckets as this report — coverage/mapping and tracker health/telemetry — plus a couple of diagnostics used to work out the Voyagers' status wording and telemetry calibration.

6.Open items

Two refinements remain, both about sharpening the numbers rather than new findings:

JUMPA few absurd "teleport" speeds (tens of thousands of km/h) are same-timestamp duplicate packets from the stream, not GPS glitches — suppress sub-2-second steps so only real bad fixes remain.
DROPOUTAuto-classify each silence as tracker fault vs coverage hole by cross-referencing the digi/igate map — if no station heard anyone in that window, it's coverage.

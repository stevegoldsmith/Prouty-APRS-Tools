APRS: Coverage, Tracking & Telemetry

What the captured APRS packet logs tell us about radio coverage, where the rovers went, and how the trackers behaved — across the 2026 Prouty Ultimate (Fri Jul 10) and Bike Event (Sat Jul 11). Two bodies of work: coverage & mapping, and tracker health & telemetry.

Scope & method

Every APRS packet heard during the event was captured to a local database as it arrived. After the event we replayed that capture to answer three questions: where our digipeater and igate network had coverage, where the rovers went, and how the trackers themselves performed.

The analysis runs against an explicit roster of the 24 Prouty rover callsigns (fixed digipeaters and igates — N1GMC-1/2, the W1FN sites, WX1NH-10 and the like — are excluded, and treated separately as infrastructure). The capture stream carries the wider APRS-IS feed, so naming the roster explicitly is what keeps distant stations and infrastructure out of the rover numbers.

One capture caveat throughout: the capture pipeline itself went silent once, for about 11 minutes on Saturday (15:03–15:14 UTC). Any apparent silence from a rover in that window is ours, not the tracker's.

Coverage & gaps

Coverage is mapped from the path of every packet: a fixed station is credited with a packet when it digipeated it (CALL*) or gated it to the internet directly off the air (qAR/qAO). We plot the transmitting rover's position for each such packet, so each receiver's dot cloud is the real-world footprint of what it could hear; holes in a cloud are that station's coverage gaps. Each of the seven fixed stations is drawn in its own colour, with traffic the stations relayed from one another separated out so it does not masquerade as field coverage.

Dropouts are mostly coverage, not tracker faults — but not yet separable. Every rover logged many multi-minute silences, scattered across the day rather than synchronised. One cluster around 15:02–15:14 Saturday lines up with the known capture gap and is ours. The rest are not yet attributable to terrain versus tracker: doing that requires cross-referencing this coverage data (did any station hear anyone from that area at that time?), which is a planned refinement, not a finished result.

Where the rovers went

Each rover's track is rebuilt from its own position beacons and exported as a KMZ for Google Earth, with a dot at every received packet so the spacing of dots along a road shows where coverage was dense versus sparse. New tracker functionality — GPS lock reporting

This year's W1CMS rovers carried Microsat APRS Voyager units, which do something the older trackers do not: they announce their own GPS state on the air, sending "GPS Lock Lost" when they lose the satellites and "GPS Locked" when they reacquire. That turns a guessing game into a measurement — we can state when a rover went dark, roughly where, and for how long, by pairing the two statuses. Only the five Voyagers report this; the rest of the fleet cannot, which is why this section covers those five alone.

Across the event the five Voyagers reported 27 GPS-loss episodes, nearly all brief:

W1CMS-11 holds the operationally interesting losses: four ran 8–16 minutes and all clustered in the higher-elevation northern section in the afternoon — a clear terrain-shadowing signature, the kind of place a rover appears stuck on the map while still moving. W1CMS-8 lost lock most often (nine times) but almost always recovered within seconds, which reads more like an install or antenna issue than terrain — and its battery trend below points the same way.

Because the Voyagers self-report, a frozen position without an accompanying "GPS Lock Lost" now points somewhere other than the sky — a wedged tracker, a timing issue, or a stale buffer. The anomaly map shows exactly where on the course each dropout happened, which is the input for deciding where a fill-in digi would help most next year.

Battery telemetry

About half the fleet beacons battery voltage — the five Voyagers via analog telemetry (T#, channel 1 = input voltage at 0.075 V/count), and several others by putting voltage in their position comments (decoded at ~0.1 V/count). The remaining rovers (AB1XQ-9, AC1TS-9, AE1H-9, K1EHZ-9, N0JSR-9, N1EMF-9, N1KRB-9, W1CMS-12, W1HS-9, W1HS-13, W1TGA-9, NA1T-8) carry no telemetry.

The fleet was healthy on power — everything sat in the normal 12–15 V band for a vehicle system (alternator up near 14.5–15, engine-off resting ~12.5). Two call-outs:

KC1LFU-1 dipped to 10.83 V early (08:20) — the only rover to cross the 11.5 V low-battery line. It recovered to ~12.5 V, so most likely an engine-off dip, but it is the one genuine low-voltage moment in the fleet.

W1CMS-8 is the vehicle to check: lowest Voyager minimum (12.15 V), trending down with no strong charging peak — and it had the most GPS losses and a frozen episode. Low or unstable supply voltage can itself make a tracker's GPS and RF flaky, so the charging/wiring on that install is worth an eyeball before next year; the voltage trend and the GPS behaviour point at the same suspect.

A real GPS fault — NA1T-8

One rover genuinely misbehaved beyond coverage. NA1T-8 sent null-island (0, 0) position fixes — an invalid GPS reading — and a 10-minute frozen stretch mid-event. Its reported 17,571 km "distance" and its wild implied speeds are entirely artefacts of the tracker jumping between 0,0 and its real position; its true track is short. This is the one tracker whose own output, not coverage, was at fault.

Tooling & open items

All of the analysis is reproducible from the capture database with a small set of standalone Python tools, kept in a git repository. The tools split into the same two buckets as this page — coverage/mapping (digi_kmz, prouty_kmz, add_packet_dots, aprs_gap_check) and tracker health/telemetry (tracker_health, telemetry_decode) — plus diagnostics used to work out the Voyagers' status wording and telemetry calibration.

JUMP noise — fixed.Absurd "teleport" speeds were same-timestamp duplicate packets from the feed; sub-2-second steps are now suppressed, leaving only genuine bad fixes (e.g. NA1T-8's null-island jumps).
DROPOUT classification — open.Auto-tag each silence as tracker fault vs coverage hole by cross-referencing the digi/igate reception data — if no station heard anyone in that window, it is coverage.
